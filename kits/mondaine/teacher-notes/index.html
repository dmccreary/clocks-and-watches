    Notes for Teachers

    These notes are for teachers, parents, and anyone who wants the technical story behind the Railway Clock Kit: which idea each lab teaches, how the face is drawn without flicker, how the stop-and-go is timed, and what has and has not been tested on real hardware. The lab pages themselves are written for students around 11 years old.

    What has been tested

    Every screen picture in the lab pages was made by running that lab's real code in a desktop simulator of the GC9B72 display. The simulator runs the kit's own lib/gc9b72.py driver, decodes the SPI commands it sends, and keeps the pixels, so the pictures show exactly what the program draws. The checks listed at the end of this page run all sixteen labs and test the timing and the drawing.

    What the simulator cannot tell you is how fast a real board draws. The wiring, the display driver (lib/gc9b72.py, unchanged), wifi_time.py, and lab 00 come from the Chrono Smart Clock Kit, whose labs have run on a real Pico 2 W and panel. The new code in this kit (lib/mondaine.py and labs 01 to 15) has been run in the simulator, but not yet on a board. Run lab 10 on yours: its "frames a second" line is the first real measurement of how the face performs.

    Sourcing Parts

    The parts are the same as the Chrono Smart Clock Kit, wired the same way, so everything on its sourcing page applies: where to buy the Pico, the display, the cable, and the buttons. The kit works with a Pico W or a Pico 2 W. It needs the W for WiFi, but not the 2. See Pico W or Pico 2 W? below for the difference you may notice.

    The Lab Sequence

    Each lab adds one idea to the ones before it. The first column links to the student page.

    Lab File New idea What students see
    00 00-blink-onboard-led.py import, Pin, while True, sleep The onboard LED blinks
    01 01-hello-display.py Modules, function calls, screen coordinates (y points down) Text, a dot, and a ring on white
    02 02-dial-dots.py Variables, for and range, sine and cosine Twelve dots in a circle
    03 03-hour-markers.py Writing functions with def, a design unit Twelve heavy bars
    04 04-minute-markers.py % (remainder), if / else The full 60-bar dial
    05 05-black-hands.py Time to angles, tuples, many-argument functions Black hands at 10:10
    06 06-red-second-hand.py Draw order (layers) The finished face, frozen
    07 07-read-the-time.py time.localtime(), tuple unpacking, using a module The face at the real time
    08 08-wifi-sync-time.py WiFi, NTP, time zones, secrets kept out of code The time, set from the internet
    09 09-tick-tock.py while loop, "only act on change" A ticking red hand
    10 10-smooth-sweep.py ticks_ms, ticks_diff, fractions, pacing a loop A gliding red hand, and a frame rate
    11 11-stop-at-twelve.py Phases of a cycle, named constants The red hand waits at 12
    12 12-minute-jump.py Easing, clamping with min / max, returning values The minute hand hops during the wait
    13 13-button-test.py Digital inputs, pull-ups, a list of tuples Three circles that fill when pressed
    14 14-set-time.py Classes, debounce, hold-to-repeat, program modes Setting the time by hand
    15 15-mondaine-clock.py Assembling parts, housekeeping, main.py The finished clock

    Labs 02 to 06 each carry their own copy of the small functions they use (point(), bar(), hand()), so a student can read and change a single file. From lab 07 on, the finished face lives in lib/mondaine.py, and the labs import it. That mirrors how the Chrono kit packages its watch parts, and it keeps labs 09 to 15 short enough to read in one sitting.

    The Design

    The face follows the proportions of the Swiss Federal Railways (SBB) station clock. Every size is a multiple of one unit, a, equal to 1/100 of the dial's diameter, so the whole face scales by changing one number.

    Part Size in a
    Five-minute bar (12 of them) from 36.5 to 48.5 out, 3.5 wide
    Minute bar (48 of them) from 45 to 48.5 out, 1.4 wide
    Hour hand 32 forward, 12 behind; 5.2 wide at the tip, 6.4 at the tail
    Minute hand 46 forward, 12 behind; 3.6 wide at the tip, 5.2 at the tail
    Second hand stem 1.4 wide; from 16.5 behind the pin to 31.25 out
    Second hand disc 10.5 across, centered 31.25 out

    Three relationships make the face fit together, and students can spot them in the pictures:

    • The disc's outer edge is 31.25 + 5.25 = 36.5 a out, exactly where the five-minute bars start. The disc touches a bar when it points at one.
    • The minute hand's tip reaches 46 a, overlapping the outer bars so the tip and a bar read as one black stroke.
    • The disc is three times as wide as a five-minute bar (10.5 = 3 × 3.5).

    The kit uses A = 3.4 pixels, which puts the outer end of the bars at 48.5 × 3.4 = 165 px. The glass is visible out to SAFE_RADIUS = 168, so the bars sit just inside the bezel. The whole screen is painted white, so the dial looks as if it fills the glass. The second hand's red is color565(235, 0, 0), a traffic-sign red, set as RAIL_RED in config.py. Pure RED (0xF800) glows too harshly against white.

    The face has no logo, and the code makes no use of any brand name. See About the Design.

    The Stop-and-Go Timing

    Each minute is 60,000 ms long:

    Milliseconds into the minute Red hand Minute and hour hands
    0 to 58,500 glides 0° to 360° at a steady 6.15° a second at rest
    58,500 to 58,650 at 12 at rest
    58,650 to 59,370 at 12 the hop: one minute (6°) and half a degree, eased
    59,370 to 60,000 at 12 at rest

    The constants are SWEEP_MS, ADVANCE_START_MS, and ADVANCE_MS in lib/mondaine.py. The pause is not a separate setting: it is whatever is left of the minute after SWEEP_MS.

    The hop uses smootherstep(x) = 6x⁵ − 15x⁴ + 10x³ (Ken Perlin's improvement on the older 3x² − 2x³ smoothstep). It has zero speed and zero acceleration at both ends, so the hand starts and lands without a visible jolt. The minute hand and the hour hand share the same eased progress, so they stay in proportion all the way through.

    The hands are continuous across the top of the minute: at 59,999 ms the minute hand is at (minute + 1) × 6 degrees, and at 0 ms of the next minute it is at the same angle. check_mondaine.py tests this at several hour boundaries, including 11:59 to 12:00 and 23:59 to 00:00.

    Counting milliseconds

    The Pico's real-time clock (RTC) only counts whole seconds, so mondaine.SweepClock makes finer time:

    1. sync() waits for the RTC's seconds to change, then reads time.ticks_ms(). Now it knows the millisecond counter's value at the start of a known second.
    2. now() adds ticks_diff(ticks_ms(), tick0) to that second, and carries over into minutes and hours.

    The RTC and the millisecond counter should both run from the board's crystal, so they stay in step to within a few milliseconds a minute. To be sure no error ever builds up, lab 12 and lab 15 call sync() once a minute, at 59,400 ms, when every hand is at rest. sync() waits for the next RTC tick, which is the top of the minute, so the red hand sets off exactly when the RTC rolls over. If the RTC is changed (lab 14's set modes, or a nightly WiFi sync), the next sync() simply follows it. No special case is needed, because the hands are always computed from the hour and minute the clock reports plus the milliseconds into the minute.

    How the Face Moves Without Flicker

    This display has no frame buffer: every pixel drawn is sent over SPI, and every drawing command has a fixed cost on top of its pixels (the driver sets a window, then sends the pixels). On the Pico 2 W, a full-screen fill takes about 131 ms, so redrawing the whole face more than seven times a second is out of the question, and it would flicker anyway.

    The Chrono kit's analog face avoids flicker by erasing a hand in the background color and then repairing whatever the erase cut through. That works there because the dial is laid out so that only a few things can be damaged. It does not work here: the red hand lies on top of both black hands, and the black hands lie on top of the outer bars. Erasing any one of them would cut a hole in the others.

    So Face.show() never erases. It works in three steps:

    1. Find what changed. Each hand is stored as a shape: the left and right pixel edge on each row it covers. For each hand that moved, compare the old and new edges row by row. A hand that moved less than one pixel has an identical shape and costs nothing. A row whose edges did not change is skipped.
    2. Decide the color of every changed pixel by looking at the whole scene from the bottom up: white dial, the 60 bars, the hour hand, the minute hand, the red stem, the red disc. The topmost thing that covers a pixel wins. Bars are only checked on rows near the rim, because nothing else can reach them.
    3. Send the changed pixels in bands. A drawing command costs about the same whether it carries 10 pixels or 600. So rows that changed are grouped, 16 at a time, into a rectangle that is filled in a scratch buffer and sent with one blit_buffer() call.

    Every pixel is therefore written once, with the color it should end up with.

    What it costs

    Measured in the simulator, counting the commands the driver is asked to send:

    Operation Drawing commands Pixels sent
    draw_dial(): fill, then 60 bars 914 lines, plus one fill 9,610 in the bars
    First draw of the three hands 413 about 8,100
    Worst frame of the red hand's sweep (any angle) 12 about 6,000
    Worst frame of the minute hop (14 steps) 13 about 3,800

    Drawing one row at a time, the same sweep frame costs about 340 commands. Banding is why the hands can be redrawn many times a second.

    check_mondaine.py proves the result is exact. It moves the hands through more than 1,100 frames (full laps of the red hand, the hop through all 60 minutes, 80 random jumps, and the disc passing every five-minute bar) and compares the screen with one drawn from scratch at the same angles. They are identical, pixel for pixel.

    Pico W or Pico 2 W?

    Both work. The pin layout is the same. The difference is speed: the Pico 2 W's RP2350 chip has hardware floating-point math and runs at 150 MHz, while the Pico W's RP2040 does floating-point in software at 133 MHz. Most of the time in a frame is spent in Python working out edges and colors, not on the SPI wire, so expect the Pico W to reach a lower frame rate than the Pico 2 W. Neither has been measured yet (see the note at the top).

    The program never depends on the frame rate. Each frame asks the clock where the hands should be right now, so a slower board shows bigger steps, never a hand that runs late. Lab 10 prints the rate every five seconds. If it is low and you want smoother motion, these are the dials, in order of effect:

    • BAUDRATE in config.py only matters if the display shows specks; see the Chrono notes on SPI speed.
    • BAND in lib/mondaine.py (16 rows). Smaller bands send fewer pixels per command but need more commands. Larger bands do the opposite.
    • FRAME_MS in labs 10, 11, 12, and 15 (50 ms). It is a target; a board that cannot keep up simply runs flat out.

    Memory

    Face keeps a scratch buffer of BAND × 360 × 2 = 11,520 bytes, three rows of 720 bytes (one per color) to copy pixels from, four corner points for each of the 60 bars, and the row edges of the four moving shapes. That is a small fraction of a Pico 2 W's 436 KB of heap. A Pico W has 264 KB of RAM in all, and it should still fit with room to spare. If you ever see a MemoryError, lower BAND to 8, which halves the scratch buffer.

    Buttons and Modes

    Lab 14 builds its own small Button class so students can see bounce and hold-to-repeat solved. Lab 15 uses lib/buttons.py, the same class plus two more tricks it needs: interrupts, so a quick tap is not lost while the loop is busy drawing, and short_or_long(), so one button can do two jobs. The class is copied from the Chrono kit's watchparts.py.

    Button Run mode Set hour / set minute
    MODE, short press next mode next mode
    MODE, hold 1 s set the clock from the internet (ignored)
    UP (nothing) forward one step; hold to repeat
    DOWN (nothing) back one step; hold to repeat

    Entering a set mode freezes the display at the RTC's hour and minute, with the red hand parked at 12 and a yellow label below the center. Changing the minute zeroes the seconds. Leaving set mode repaints the dial (which wipes the label), re-syncs the sweep clock to the RTC, and the clock runs again.

    Making the Clock Start by Itself

    Lab 15 is the whole clock. Save a copy of it on the Pico as main.py, and the Pico runs it every time it is powered. upload-code.sh copies the lib folder, config.py, secrets.py (if you made one), and every lab to the Pico. It does not copy the simulator, which lives outside the kit folder.

    Quit Thonny before running upload-code.sh

    Only one program can use the Pico's serial port at a time. If Thonny is connected, mpremote fails with a message that the port is in use.

    Troubleshooting

    Problem Likely cause Fix
    Screen stays dark Wiring, or VCC on a 5 V pin Check the table in How It Is Wired. Run lab 00 first to prove the board works.
    Ring or bars cut off at the edge The bezel covers more of your glass Lower SAFE_RADIUS in config.py, and UNIT in lib/mondaine.py (try 3.2)
    Specks, or torn frames Long or messy display wires Set BAUDRATE = 12_000_000 in config.py
    Red hand moves in big steps A low frame rate (see lab 10's report) Normal on a Pico W; see Pico W or Pico 2 W?. The hand is still in the right place.
    MemoryError on a Pico W Too little free RAM Set BAND = 8 in lib/mondaine.py; remove labs you don't need from the board
    Red hand does not stop at 12 The Pico's time is wrong, or the program is an older copy Check the NAME vX.Y line in the shell; run lab 08
    The minute hand hops at the wrong moment The RTC was changed while running It re-aligns itself within one minute, at the next rest
    Time off by exactly one hour Time zone or daylight saving Check TIMEZONE_HOURS and USE_US_DST in config.py
    Time starts at midnight The Pico lost power, and no WiFi sync ran Run lab 08, or lab 15 with SYNC_WITH_WIFI = True
    WiFi failed Wrong name or password, or a 5 GHz network Check secrets.py; use a 2.4 GHz network
    One press moves the hand more than once Contact bounce Check that lab 14's DEBOUNCE_MS is 40 and that the button leg touches GND
    The SET HOUR label is still showing The program is stuck in a set mode Press MODE until the label disappears

    Checks and Pictures

    The simulator is in the repository at src/display-simulators/gc9b72. Two scripts there belong to this kit:

    • checks/check_mondaine.py runs all sixteen labs, tests the face's geometry and the stop-and-go timing, proves that incremental repainting matches a full redraw, and reports the cost of each frame in drawing commands.
    • render_mondaine_docs.py regenerates every picture in this kit's img folder from the labs' own code. Run it after changing a lab, then look at the results.

    Both run with only Python 3.10 or newer and Pillow. No board is needed.
